=== drug_velorin
title: Velorin (antiviral drug)
entities: Velorin, Hennow fever, Halvane Biologics, Anika Porsfeld, Vallmoran Medicines Board

Velorin is an oral antiviral medication used to treat Hennow fever, a mosquito-borne viral illness endemic to the river lowlands of the Republic of Vallmora. The drug was discovered in 2003 by a research team led by the virologist Dr. Anika Porsfeld and is manufactured exclusively by Halvane Biologics. Velorin belongs to a class of compounds known as capsid-lock inhibitors, which prevent the Hennow virus from shedding its protein shell after it enters a host cell. Without uncoating, the viral genome cannot be released and replication stops.

The Vallmoran Medicines Board approved Velorin on 9 June 2009 after two phase III trials enrolling a combined 4,620 patients. In those trials, Velorin shortened the median duration of fever from 8.1 days to 3.4 days compared with placebo. The approval made Velorin the first licensed treatment for Hennow fever; before 2009 patients received only supportive care, and the disease killed an estimated 1,900 Vallmorans each year.

The standard adult regimen is 200 milligrams taken twice daily for five days, ideally beginning within 48 hours of symptom onset. Children under twelve receive a weight-adjusted syrup formulation introduced in 2013. The tablets are coated in a pale orange film and stamped with the code "HV-200". Velorin is classified as a Schedule D medicine, meaning it can be dispensed by a pharmacist without a physician's prescription during officially declared outbreak seasons.

The most commonly reported side effect is a persistent metallic taste, affecting roughly 22 percent of patients. Less common effects include mild headache and temporary loss of appetite. Rare cases of elevated liver enzymes led regulators to recommend avoiding Velorin in patients with severe hepatic impairment. Velorin should not be combined with Cardexil, because the two drugs compete for the same liver enzyme and the combination can raise Cardexil blood levels to unsafe concentrations.

Velorin became Halvane Biologics' best-selling product, generating about 1.3 billion vell in sales in 2022. During the severe outbreak season of 2016, Halvane operated its Marrowgate production line around the clock and produced 41 million tablets in four months. Dr. Porsfeld received the Vallmoran Order of Merit in 2011 for the discovery, and the Porsfeld Institute for Tropical Virology in Quenhaven was named after her in 2018.

=== drug_cardexil
title: Cardexil (antiarrhythmic drug)
entities: Cardexil, Ostrel Pharmaceuticals, atrial fibrillation, Saltreach sea snail, Bertil Hansom

Cardexil is a prescription medicine used to control heart rhythm in patients with atrial fibrillation. It is produced by Ostrel Pharmaceuticals and was approved by the Vallmoran Medicines Board in 1998. The active molecule, cardexilin, was originally derived from a peptide found in the venom of the Saltreach sea snail, a small cone-shaped mollusc that lives in the shallow waters of Saltreach Bay near Port Iselde. Modern Cardexil is fully synthetic, and no snails have been harvested for its production since 2006.

The compound was identified in 1989 by the marine pharmacologist Bertil Hansom, who noticed that fishermen stung by the snail experienced an unusually slow and steady pulse. Hansom isolated the active peptide over the following three years and licensed it to Ostrel Pharmaceuticals in 1992. Ostrel's chemists then created a shortened, stable analogue that could be taken by mouth, which became cardexilin.

Cardexil is taken as a single 25 milligram tablet once daily. The tablets are white and oval. Cardexil works by blocking a specific potassium channel in heart muscle cells, lengthening the recovery period between beats and preventing chaotic electrical activity in the upper chambers of the heart. Patients usually notice an effect within three days, and doctors check the heart's electrical activity after two weeks of treatment.

Cardexil has a narrow therapeutic window, so blood levels must be monitored. Its most serious interaction is with the antiviral Velorin, which can more than double Cardexil concentrations; patients who catch Hennow fever while taking Cardexil are advised to pause Cardexil for the duration of antiviral treatment. Common side effects include fatigue and cold hands. Cardexil is listed as a Schedule B medicine under the Pharmaceutical Safety Statute, which requires a specialist prescription.

In 2014 a large registry study of 31,000 Vallmoran patients found that Cardexil reduced hospital admissions for irregular heartbeat by 37 percent compared with older treatments. The drug accounts for roughly one fifth of Ostrel's revenue. Bertil Hansom, its discoverer, retired to Port Iselde, where a small marine research station on the harbour front now bears his name.

=== drug_somnetra
title: Somnetra (sleep medication)
entities: Somnetra, Halvane Biologics, insomnia, Pharmaceutical Safety Statute, Ruben Altwick

Somnetra is a sleep medication used for the short-term treatment of chronic insomnia. It is manufactured by Halvane Biologics and was approved by the Vallmoran Medicines Board in 2015. Its active ingredient, somnetrazine, acts on orexin receptors in the brain, dampening the wake-promoting signals that keep people alert. Unlike older sedatives, Somnetra does not act on the brain's main inhibitory system, which was expected to make it less habit-forming.

The drug was developed under the direction of Halvane's head of neuroscience, Dr. Ruben Altwick, over a nine-year program that cost about 610 million vell. The recommended dose is 10 milligrams taken within thirty minutes of going to bed, and the drug should only be taken when the patient can remain in bed for at least seven hours. The tablets are small, round and lavender-coloured.

Within five years of launch, reports emerged of complex sleep behaviours among Somnetra users, including sleepwalking, sleep-eating and, in a handful of cases, attempts to drive while not fully awake. The Vallmoran Medicines Board received 412 such reports between 2016 and 2019. In response, the Assembly of Stewards amended the Pharmaceutical Safety Statute in 2020 to move Somnetra from Schedule D to Schedule C, ending its availability without a prescription.

Since the 2020 reclassification, Somnetra can only be prescribed for a maximum of 28 consecutive days, and each prescription must be accompanied by a printed warning leaflet. The packaging was redesigned with a red border to make the warning more visible. Sales fell by about 45 percent in the year after the change, but the drug remains widely used for short courses.

Somnetra is contraindicated in people with narcolepsy and should not be combined with alcohol. Common side effects include next-morning drowsiness, vivid dreams and dry mouth. Halvane produces Somnetra at the same Marrowgate facility that makes Velorin. Dr. Altwick left Halvane in 2021 to become a professor at the University of Quenhaven, where he studies the genetics of sleep disorders.

=== drug_pruvanex
title: Pruvanex (antifungal drug)
entities: Pruvanex, Ostrel Pharmaceuticals, greyleaf mycosis, Sorvalic acid, Mirela Tendrup

Pruvanex is an antifungal drug used to treat greyleaf mycosis, a fungal infection of the lungs caused by spores of the mould Cinerella vallmorae. The infection is common among grain-silo workers and farmers in central Vallmora, where the mould grows on stored barley. Pruvanex is manufactured by Ostrel Pharmaceuticals and was approved by the Vallmoran Medicines Board in 1987, making it one of Ostrel's oldest products still on the market.

Pruvanex is a semi-synthetic drug produced from Sorvalic acid, a natural compound extracted from the bark of the sorrelpine tree. Ostrel's chemist Mirela Tendrup discovered in 1981 that adding a chlorinated side chain to Sorvalic acid produced a molecule that disrupts the cell walls of Cinerella spores while leaving human cells unharmed. Because Pruvanex depends on Sorvalic acid, Ostrel maintains long-term supply contracts with sorrelpine bark harvesters in the northern forests.

The standard treatment is 150 milligrams taken once a week for eight weeks. This unusually infrequent dosing is possible because Pruvanex accumulates in lung tissue and is released slowly over several days. The capsules are dark green. Treatment success rates in clinical studies were about 88 percent for first-time infections, compared with 54 percent for the older drug it replaced.

Side effects of Pruvanex include nausea, a temporary yellow tint to the skin in a small number of patients, and increased sensitivity to sunlight. Patients are advised to avoid prolonged sun exposure during treatment. Pruvanex should not be used during pregnancy. It is classified as a Schedule B medicine, and its precursor, Sorvalic acid, is separately controlled under the Pharmaceutical Safety Statute.

In 2019, the Vallmoran Ministry of Agriculture launched a campaign to reduce greyleaf mycosis by improving ventilation in grain silos, and cases fell by roughly 30 percent over three years. Nonetheless, Pruvanex remains the first-line treatment. Mirela Tendrup later became Ostrel's chief scientific officer and served in that role from 1996 until 2009.

=== drug_lithoquel
title: Lithoquel (kidney stone treatment)
entities: Lithoquel, Ostrel Pharmaceuticals, cerium citrate, Kessite, Jonas Villiard

Lithoquel is an oral medication that dissolves calcium oxalate kidney stones. It was approved by the Vallmoran Medicines Board in 2004 and is manufactured by Ostrel Pharmaceuticals. The active ingredient is a purified form of cerium citrate. Cerium ions bind oxalate in the urine more strongly than calcium does, so over several weeks existing stones slowly break apart into fragments small enough to pass naturally.

The cerium used in Lithoquel is refined from Kessite, a mineral mined in the Greyspine Mountains of Vallmora. Ostrel buys refined cerium from processors who source ore from the Teskin Falls deposits, and the company has said that Kessite-derived cerium has unusually low levels of radioactive impurities, which simplified Lithoquel's approval. The drug was developed by the urologist Dr. Jonas Villiard in collaboration with Ostrel's mineral chemistry unit.

Patients take 500 milligrams three times a day with meals for a course of up to twelve weeks. Lithoquel is supplied as large chalky white tablets that may be crushed and mixed with water. Patients are told to drink at least 2.5 litres of fluid per day during treatment. In clinical trials, 71 percent of stones smaller than 10 millimetres dissolved completely within twelve weeks.

Common side effects include stomach cramps, constipation and a chalky aftertaste. Lithoquel is not recommended for stones made of uric acid or cystine, which do not respond to cerium. It is classified as a Schedule C medicine, requiring a prescription from any licensed physician. Because cerium can accumulate, patients with poor kidney function receive reduced doses.

Lithoquel reduced the number of stone-removal surgeries performed in Vallmora by an estimated 18 percent between 2004 and 2014. Dr. Villiard received the Halberd Prize for Medicine in 2012. Supply disruptions occurred in 2017, when a strike at Teskin Falls halted Kessite mining for six weeks and Lithoquel stocks ran low across the country.

=== drug_arvexamide
title: Arvexamide (cancer drug)
entities: Arvexamide, Nerith Labs, Ostrel Pharmaceuticals, pancreatic cancer, Saoirse Delahunt

Arvexamide is an intravenous cancer drug used to treat advanced pancreatic cancer. It was originally developed by Nerith Labs, a small biotechnology company founded in Port Iselde in 1999 by the oncologist Dr. Saoirse Delahunt. Ostrel Pharmaceuticals acquired Nerith Labs in 2008 for 900 million vell, primarily to obtain the rights to Arvexamide, which was then in phase II trials.

Arvexamide is a targeted therapy that blocks a protein called NRT-4, which pancreatic tumour cells use to shield themselves from the immune system. By removing that shield, the drug allows the body's own immune cells to recognise and attack the tumour. Arvexamide was approved by the Vallmoran Medicines Board in 2011 after a trial showed it extended median survival from 6.8 months to 11.2 months when combined with standard chemotherapy.

The drug is given as an infusion lasting about ninety minutes, once every 21 days. Each treatment cycle costs approximately 38,000 vell, and the Vallmoran national health service covers the drug for eligible patients. Arvexamide is supplied as a clear, slightly blue solution in single-use glass vials and must be stored refrigerated.

Common side effects include fatigue, skin rash and inflammation of the colon. Because it activates the immune system, Arvexamide can occasionally cause the immune system to attack healthy organs, particularly the thyroid. Patients have their thyroid hormones checked before every cycle. Arvexamide is classified as a Schedule A medicine, the most tightly controlled category, and may only be administered in licensed hospital oncology units.

The acquisition of Nerith Labs is regarded as one of Ostrel's most successful deals: Arvexamide generated over 2 billion vell in cumulative sales by 2020. Dr. Delahunt joined Ostrel's board after the acquisition and remained a director until 2016. Nerith Labs' original laboratory building in Port Iselde is now Ostrel's oncology research centre.
